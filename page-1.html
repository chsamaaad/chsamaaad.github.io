A Tale of Two Cities

A Tale of Two Worlds

It was a time that pulled humanity in two opposite directions at once. To look out at the world was to see the finest heights of promise tangled up with the darkest depths of ruin. People were bursting with brilliance, yet stumbling through unbelievable folly. One day brought unshakable faith; the next arrived thick with cynical doubt. You could wake to the dazzling light of a brand-new dawn or go to sleep suffocated by gloom.

In every town square and fireside debate, folks felt they had the whole world within reach, only to look down and realize their hands were empty. Some were convinced they were marching straight through the pearly gates, while just as many seemed dead set on taking the highway to the other place. It felt messy, noisy, and chaotic—not all that different from how the world feels today, where everyone with a loud voice insists things are either the absolute best or an absolute disaster, with no room left in between.

Crowns and Blind Certainty

Across the water, the rulers sat comfortably above the squalor, entirely sure of themselves. Over in England, you had a king with a heavy, stubborn jaw and a queen with an ordinary, quiet face. Across the English Channel in France, another king held court with a jaw just as heavy, sitting beside a queen famed for her fair complexion. To the pampered nobles and wealthy lords in both palaces, the banquet was set, the wine was flowing, and the order of things felt permanent. They truly believed the world belonged to them forever, set in stone, untouchable.

Whispers of 1775

The calendar read 1775, and beneath the surface of that proud year, the ground was already trembling. While monarchs waved their royal decrees with heavy hands and declared their god-given right to everything they touched, ordinary life carried on in the shadows. The woodcutter kept chopping wood that would soon become the timber of guillotines; the farmer quietly tended the soil that would soon bury an empire. The rulers walked loud and tall, completely blind to the fact that their grand illusion was running out of time.

Retold from A Tale of Two Cities by Charles Dickens, originally published by Chapman & Hall (1859).
